The [[Banner Sea]] was once the land known as Ar or the Banner Kingdom, before the Great Cataclysm during the War in Heaven sent it to the bottom of the South Blue. According to the oldest surviving accounts, the first Ar'ans were a confederation of mountain clans forged into a single people by the warlord-king **Aruk the Unbroken**, who united the clans through conquest and introduced the "*Oath of Iron*", a vow that every Ar'an, from lowest herder to highest lord, owed a season of martial service to the king.

From this oath grew the defining institution of Ar'an life: the **Banner System**. Every settlement, every citizen, maintained its own war-banner. Their banner was their identity, their family, and their honor. each banner bore the achievements of the barer (or shame if they acted against the betterment of Ar). To lose a banner in battle was worse than death; to abandon it was to cease being Ar'an at all.

Ar's early centuries were marked by near-constant border wars, which the Ar'ans used to consolidate power and by XXXX, Ar had grown from a confederation of clans into the preeminent martial power of its region. Its legions, the **Iron Banners**, were unmatched in discipline and doctrine. Ar'an officers were studied and feared abroad, and foreign kings paid ruinous sums to hire Ar'an banner-companies as mercenaries or (more often), paid ruinous tribute to ensure they stayed home.

It was during this age that Ar's great noble houses crystallized into their final form. Chief among them:

- **House Aruk-Kel**, the royal line, claiming descent from Aruk the Unbroken himself.
- **House Varn**, masters of the eastern marches and the cavalry traditions.
- **House Arn**, the scholar-generals, keepers of the *Codex Militaris*, the accumulated strategic wisdom of Ar — and traditional advisors to the throne.
- **House Dral**, the forge-lords, whose smithies armed the Iron Banners.

House Arn occupied a unique and sometimes uneasy position in Ar'an society. While most houses swore unquestioning loyalty to the king, House Arn was not originally subjugated into the nation but was an early willing member of the confederation but maintained a long tradition of loyalty to the nation, NOT to the king. 

House Arn was also one of the few houses that would travel outside the Banner Kingdom to study the world abroad. After many long years as mercenaries and advisors in the land of Edo, the Iron Lord Arit Arn would write the Codex Militaris, often regarded as one of the preeminent tomes of the art of war.

To the south-east of Ar lay **Numinar**, a nation as devoted to the divine as Ar was to the martial. Where Ar'ans raised banner-halls, Numinarians raised temples; where Ar produced generals, Numinar produced oracles, theologians, and the priest-savants who claimed to hear the voices of the gods directly.

The two nations were natural opposites but, for centuries, natural partners. Ar had steel; Numinar had grain, gold, and the favor of the heavens. The **Concord of the Two Pillars** (XXXX) formalized what geography had already made true: Ar was the sword-arm of the world, Numinar its bread basket and praying hands. Ar'an banner-companies guarded Numinarian temple-roads; Numinarian priests blessed Ar'an banners before campaign. Intermarriage between the border provinces was common, and the great trade city on the frontier between them grew wealthy beyond measure.

It was said in those years that no two nations were less alike, and no two nations were closer.


## The War in Heaven

>*"Once, the Gods were ignorant of strife. Though they had long used both Kith & Kin to resolve their numerous disputes, direct conflicts between the old gods was once an unthinkable act. In reserving the ultimate powers of the Myst to themselves they could not conceive any threat, let alone of their own creation could ever emerge to challenge their dominance."* - Aldric Dunstan, A Guide to Æthernian History I


Before that day, the Day Heaven Cried, the heavens had not known war. The old gods, the elder powers of the first order, had ruled uncontested since the world was young, and what shattered their long peace was not a rival god but a creature of their own making. The surviving theologians of Ar'num name him only as the Deceiver, for they burned his true name from every record after the Fall; but the oldest fragments remember what he had been before he earned that title.

He was a general of the celestial host, the brightest of the servants raised nearest the thrones of the old gods, set to command the light that was said to walk before them. And it was that very nearness to the divine, the chroniclers teach, that damned him: a being fashioned to lead armies at the gods' side came, in time, to loath the very gods who formed him.

He was sent by the Morning Star, the head of the rebellion, to lead one of the most important early moves in the war. The old gods were mighty, after all, their strength was given as the first-born of the Mysts, but long had they grown dependent on the ambrosia drawn from their creation of the mortal world.  Worship was now the true source of their power, one prayer, one offering, one act of faith at a time. It was this dependency the general saw, and this dependency he moved to sever. A god of a thousand temples was a fortress; a god whose faithful lay slaughtered was a beggar. The Deceiver needed to starve the gods of the worship from their most faithful.

The well of worship from Numinar was deep, and well defended so that any attempt to destroy it would be difficult to prosecute without attention from the gods. Even though the gods would not permit the well to be struck directly, it be undone from below. As the well of Numinar was connected to the well of Ar, and Ar's well was far less defended. So the plan was hatched to poison the well of Ar, and let its rot seep upward through the roots of friendship until it poisoned Numinar itself.

### The Great Lie

The Deceiver's work was patient and total. Numinar's well of worship was too deep and too well-watched to be struck head-on; Ar's well of friendship with Numinar was much less defended, and it was through poisoning Ar that Numinar would be undone.ft.

The thrust came first. To the war-temples of Ar came omens and false oracles in a rising flood: visions showing that Numinar's priest-savants were siphoning the strength of Ar's ancestral spirits, that the honored dead in the banner-halls souls were being *drained* to fatten Numinar's harvests, that the Concord of the Two Pillars had been, from its first day, a leash disguised as a handshake. The visions were tailored, chroniclers later observed, with a craftsman's understanding of the Ar'an heart: they did not tempt Ar with conquest, they threatened Ar with betrayal. A nation whose founding oath was fidelity was told, in every temple, that its dearest friend had been faithless from the first.

To Numinar the Deceiver sent the softer poison, not to inflame, but to blind. Mirror-omens whispered that Ar's legions were massing, that the Iron Banners had been promised Numinar's temple-gold as spoils by foreign conquering kings, that the ancient Concord had bought Numinar only a slower knife. These lies were not meant to make Numinar march. They were meant to make Numinar hesitate, to make Numinar's oracles distrust the very Ar'an voices that would soon come pleading, so that when Ar's honest men (and there were honest men, right to the end) sent envoys to say *we are being lied to, help us prove it,* Numinar would hear only the confession of an enemy already arming.

Border incidents, some manufactured by the Deceiver's mortal cults, some genuine panics born of the lies, accumulated like dry tinder along the friendship the Concord had built. Every incident was read twice, once in Ar and once in Numinar, and by design neither reading matched the other. The murder of a Numinarian high oracle on Ar'an soil, a killing later understood to be the Deceiver's own work, executed by hands that believed themselves patriots, was the spark. It could not have been better chosen: to Ar, proof that Numinar's oracles had been meddling on sovereign soil; to Numinar, proof that Ar's protection of its guests was worth nothing; to both, a wound too public to walk back.

King **Torvak IV Aruk-Kel** summoned the Council of Banners and called for war. The well of Ar was exposed at last, and the Deceiver had only to wait for the faithful of two nations to poison it themselves.

### The Dissent of House Arn

One house alone stood against the tide. **Lord Kaelen Arn**, First Scholar-General and keeper of the Codex Militaris, rose before the Council and spoke the words every Ar'an child in Ar'num still learns:

> *"I have studied every war Ar has ever fought, and I tell you: this one has no author among mortals. Look at how perfectly we are provoked. Look at how precisely our fears are fed. When an enemy knows your heart better than you know it yourself, you are not marching to your war, you are marching to someone else's"*

For this, House Arn was branded craven, then heretical, then treasonous. Kaelen Arn was stripped of the Codex and his seat on the Council. Arn banner-halls were burned by mobs while city watchmen stood aside. Three of Kaelen's kin were executed on fabricated charges of Numinarian collaboration, an irony the Deceiver must have savored. The survivors of the house were banished from Ar itself, cast out beyond the nation's borders to **Ar-Numath**, a lonely fortress-harbor on a foreign coast far from the heartland, an aging garrison-port Ar had once built to guard the old sea-lanes of the Concord, and had long since ceased to care about. The Council intended it as a living grave: House Arn would rot in a salt-bitten outpost at the edge of the world, out of sight of the nation it had "betrayed."

It was this persecution that saved them.

## The Fall of Ar

The war that followed was the one of the most terrible in mortal memory, because it was never truly a mortal war. As the Iron Banners and the temple-hosts of Numinar annihilated one another across a decade of campaigns, with each nation of the faithful butchered in the mud, the old gods who had drawn their power from them guttered and dimmed, like lamps starved of oil. Battles were fought under skies that cracked and bled; whole legions were unmade by powers no doctrine in the Codex Militaris had ever contemplated.

By the war's end, the Deceiver had what he sought. Both wells were poisoned. The faith of Numinar shattered when its oracles were revealed as compromised; the strength of Ar bled out on a hundred fields, and the fat harvest of both nations' dead had gorged the Deceiver as he launched the divine campaign arm of the War in Heaven.  And then, in the final hour of the War in Heaven, the reckoning fell out of the sky upon Ar, the nation whose slaughter had fed his ascension made, in the end, the ground of his ruin.

### The Sundering

No two accounts of that day agree on everything, but all agree on this: it began with a second sun.

Survivors on the far borderlands — the only ones who survived at all — described a point of light descending over the heartland of Ar, brighter than noon, so bright that shadows fell *away* from it at midday and those who looked upon it directly were blinded where they stood. Some say it was the Deceiver himself, cast down at the very threshold of the godhood he had murdered a world to reach, his stolen divinity coming apart as he fell; some say it was the old gods' answering stroke, loosed to unmake him and unmaking all beneath him too.

It struck the capital.

The light came first, a white flash seen, it is claimed, from every nation in the world. Then the sound, which arrived at the borderlands long after the light, rolling on and on like the sky tearing loose from its hinges. A wall of burning wind flattened forests a hundred miles from the heartland and scoured the great border city down to its foundations. Above the place where the capital had been, witnesses described a vast column of fire and shadow climbing into the heavens, spreading at its crown like a colossal, blazing tree, as if the dead god's last act was to plant its own funeral pyre in the corpse of the nation that had died for it.

And then the land itself gave way.

The impact had broken something deep beneath Ar. The old chronicles say the god's fall "cracked the world's floor like a shield boss struck true." Over the hours and days that followed, the entire heartland of Ar, its capital, its banner-halls, the ancestral seats of Aruk-Kel, Varn, and Dral, its mountains, its ten thousand graves, groaned, shuddered, and sank. The sea rushed in to fill the wound. Coastal chroniclers in distant lands recorded a series of monstrous waves and a rain of grey ash that fell for a season, and wrote simply: *"The sword-arm of the world was severed, and the sea closed over it."*

Numinar was spared the Sundering itself but not its consequences; the waves, the ash-fall, and the collapse of the heavens' order broke its temple-cities, and its people scattered.

## The Present

Now the former land of Ar is no more than an underwater grave, known as the [[Banner Sea]].
