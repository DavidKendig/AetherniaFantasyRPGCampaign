## Fertility Customs & Seasonal Birth Planning

### The Agrarian Logic

Rural folk have long understood the importance of timing births to the agricultural cycle. A child born too early in winter strains dwindling food stores; one born during harvest pulls hands away from critical labor. Generations of accumulated wisdom point to an ideal window: **late Candelum through early Germinal** (roughly days 75–110).

A child born in this window arrives as:
- Winter stores still hold, ensuring adequate food for a nursing mother
- The worst cold has passed, improving infant survival
- The mother has time to recover before the demanding planting season
- The family can celebrate new life alongside the rebirth of the land

### The Conception Window

Working backwards from ideal birth dates (~266 days of gestation):

| Desired Birth | Conception Window |
|---------------|-------------------|
| Mid-Candelum (Day 75) | Late Florialis (Day 169) |
| End of Candelum (Day 90) | Early Solium (Day 184) |
| Mid-Germinal (Day 110) | Mid-Solium (Day 204) |

This places the ideal conception period in **late Florialis through mid-Solium** (roughly days 165–210)—the warmest, most abundant time of year.

### Cultural Traditions

This timing aligns beautifully with existing customs. Florialis is already the traditional month for betrothals and weddings, when young couples are matched amid flower festivals and fertility rites. The wedding night and early weeks of marriage thus fall naturally into the optimal conception window.

Common folk sayings reflect this wisdom:

> *"Wed in the flowers, bed in the sun, and the babe comes when the seeds do."*

> *"A Florialis bride brings Germinal life."*

> *"Plant your seed when the sun stands high, and reap your child when the candles burn."*

### Variations by Class

- **Peasant families** adhere closely to this schedule out of necessity. A poorly-timed birth can mean the difference between survival and starvation.
- **Noble houses** are less constrained by agricultural labor but still prefer spring births, as heirs born in the "growing time" are considered auspicious and strong.
- **Merchant families** often favor births in Messorium or Vindemial, after the summer trading season but before winter travel becomes impossible, allowing the mother to rest during the slower months.

# Fantasy Calendar System

  

A 360-day calendar with eight months of 45 days each and an eight-day week. The new year begins the day after the winter solstice.

  

---

  

## The Eight Months

  

### Glacium (Days 1–45)

From *glacies* (ice). The month of deep cold, when the world sleeps beneath frost. A time of hearth-keeping and endurance.

  

### Candelum (Days 46–90)

From *candela* (candle). Named for the festival of lights held as days slowly lengthen. Folk light candles to coax the sun's return.

  

### Germinal (Days 91–135)

From *germen* (seed/bud). The awakening month when farmers bless their seed stores and the first green shoots push through thawing earth.

  

### Florialis (Days 136–180)

From *flos* (flower). The flowering month, sacred to spirits of growth and courtship. A traditional time for betrothals.

  

### Solium (Days 181–225)

From *sol* (sun). The height of summer, containing the longest day. A month of labor, travel, and military campaigns.

  

### Messorium (Days 226–270)

From *messis* (harvest). The grain-reaping month when the fields are cut and the first fruits gathered.

  

### Vindemial (Days 271–315)

From *vindemia* (grape harvest). The month of wine-making, preserving, and preparation for winter. Markets overflow with autumn plenty.

  

### Umbralis (Days 316–360)

From *umbra* (shadow). The darkening month, when the veil between worlds thins. Ends with the solstice vigil before the new year.


## Calendar Structure

  

- **Days per year:** 360

- **Months:** 8

- **Days per month:** 45

- **Days per week:** 8

- **Weeks per month:** 5 (plus 5 extra days)

- **New Year:** Day after the winter solstice

## Holidays & Festivals

  

### Glacium (Days 1–45)

  

**The Dawning (Day 1)** — New Year's day. Bonfires are lit at dawn to "strengthen" the newborn sun. Debts are forgiven, quarrels settled, and oaths renewed. Feasting lasts through the night.

  

**Hearthwatch (Day 23)** — A solemn mid-winter observance. Families gather to share stories of ancestors who survived hard winters. The eldest lights a candle for each generation remembered. To let your hearth fire die on Hearthwatch invites ill fortune.

  

---

  

### Candelum (Days 46–90)

  

**Luminaria (Day 50)** — The Festival of Lights. Every window blazes with candles to guide the sun back to strength. Children carry lanterns through the streets, and young men compete to build the tallest bonfire. The night ends with a procession to the local temple or sacred grove.

  

**The Quickening (Day 80)** — A day marking the first perceptible lengthening of daylight. Pregnant women receive blessings, ewes are checked for lambs, and the first hopeful prayers for spring are offered.

  

---

  

### Germinal (Days 91–135)

  

**Sowing Day (Day 95)** — Priests bless seed stores, and the first symbolic furrows are cut. Work is forbidden after midday; the afternoon is spent in community feasting and dancing to ensure the earth's fertility.

  

**The Vernal Threshold (Day 100)** — Marking the balance point when day begins to overtake night. Doorways are decorated with green branches. Young people leap over low fires for luck and fertility. A time for new ventures and contracts.

  

---

  

### Florialis (Days 136–180)

  

**Floral Crown (Day 145)** — The great courtship festival. Unmarried youth wear flower crowns and exchange blooms with those who catch their eye. Betrothals announced on this day are considered especially blessed.

  

**The Lover's Chase (Day 165)** — A raucous holiday where traditional roles reverse. Women "hunt" men through fields and forests in a ritualized game. Those caught exchange tokens and often wed by summer's end. Alehouses overflow late into the warm night.

  

---

  

### Solium (Days 181–225)

  

**Solstice Triumph (Day 181)** — The longest day. Grand processions honor the sun at its zenith. Military victories are celebrated, rulers hold court publicly, and oaths sworn at high noon are considered unbreakable. Bonfires burn until dawn.

  

**The Golden Market (Day 210)** — A week-long trade fair (Days 210–217) drawing merchants from distant lands. Contracts signed during the Golden Market carry extra legal weight. Entertainers, competitions, and exotic goods fill every town square.

  

---

  

### Messorium (Days 226–270)

  

**First Sheaf (Day 230)** — The first grain is ceremonially cut and woven into a sacred figure, kept until next year's harvest. Communities share bread baked from the new flour. To refuse a neighbor bread on First Sheaf is deeply shameful.

  

**The Reaper's Rest (Day 260)** — Marks the end of the main harvest. Laborers are released early, debts to workers are settled, and a great communal feast celebrates the year's bounty. Competitions of strength and skill determine the "Harvest King."

  

---

  

### Vindemial (Days 271–315)

  

**The Treading (Day 280)** — The grape harvest festival. Communities gather to crush grapes by foot, accompanied by music and revelry. The first taste of new wine is offered to the gods before anyone drinks. A day of joyful, messy celebration.

  

**Autumnal Veil (Day 295)** — The balance point when night overtakes day. A quieter, reflective holiday. Families take stock of their stores and give thanks. Temples hold services for safe passage through the dark months ahead.

  

---

  

### Umbralis (Days 316–360)

  

**Night of Shades (Day 330)** — When the veil between worlds is thinnest. Families set empty places at table for departed loved ones. Masked processions wind through streets to confuse malevolent spirits. Children receive treats to "sweeten the dead." Doors are marked with protective sigils.

  

**The Vigil (Days 358–360)** — The final three days of the year, a solemn period of reflection, fasting, and prayer. Fires are extinguished on Day 358 and relit only at dawn on the Dawning. The longest night (Day 360) is spent in community gatherings, awaiting the sun's return together.
