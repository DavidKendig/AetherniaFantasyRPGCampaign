To the east of [[Ar]] lay **Numinar**, a nation as devoted to the divine as Ar was to the martial. Where Ar'ans raised banner-halls, Numinarians raised temples; where Ar produced generals, Numinar produced oracles, theologians, and the priest-savants who claimed to hear the voices of the gods directly. It was, for a thousand years, the deepest well of worship in the mortal world, and it is now the most desolate ground upon it.

>*"There is no ruin so complete as a holy ruin. When a fortress falls, men mourn the walls; when a temple falls, they mourn the god. Numinar lost both in a single generation, and what crawled in to fill the empty places was worse than either."* — Aldric Dunstan, A Guide to Æthernian History III

## The Land of a Thousand Temples

Numinar's power was never steel. Its soil was black and endlessly fertile, watered by the rivers that spilled down from the eastern highlands, and its granaries fed nations that could not feed themselves. But grain was only the visible half of Numinar's wealth. The other half was faith.

The Numinarians had raised the practice of worship to something between an art and a science. Their **priest-savants** did not merely pray; they charted the moods of the heavens, catalogued the omens, and tended the great temple-cities as one might tend a vast and delicate machine. Chief among these was the sacred capital of **Numath-Vael**, the City of Ten Thousand Bells, whose towers were said to ring of their own accord when the gods drew near. It was here that the deepest well of worship in all the world was tended, an inexhaustible spring of ambrosia upon which the old gods themselves had grown dependent.

For centuries Numinar and Ar were natural opposites and natural partners. Ar had steel; Numinar had grain, gold, and the favor of the heavens. The **Concord of the Two Pillars** formalized what geography had already made true: Ar was the sword-arm of the world, Numinar its bread basket and praying hands. Ar'an banner-companies guarded Numinarian temple-roads; Numinarian priests blessed Ar'an banners before campaign. It was said in those years that no two nations were less alike, and no two nations were closer.

That closeness was the very thing that killed them both.

## The Poisoning

The full account of the treachery belongs to the history of [[Ar]], for it was Ar that was struck first and Ar that was made the weapon. But the target, from the beginning, was Numinar.

The Deceiver, the fallen general of the celestial host, had studied the wells of the world and found Numinar's the deepest and the best defended. It could not be struck head-on; the gods watched it too closely. So he struck instead at the friendship between the two nations, poisoning Ar with false oracles and manufactured omens, letting the rot seep eastward through the roots of the Concord until it reached the well he truly wanted.

To Numinar the Deceiver sent the softer poison, not to inflame but to blind. Mirror-omens whispered that Ar's legions were massing, that the Iron Banners had been promised Numinar's temple-gold as spoils. These lies were not meant to make Numinar march. They were meant to make Numinar *hesitate*, so that when Ar's honest men sent envoys pleading *we are being lied to, help us prove it,* Numinar's oracles would hear only the confession of an enemy already arming.

The terrible irony of Numinar's fall is that its greatest strength became the instrument of its ruin. A nation that heard the gods directly was a nation that could be made to hear anything, and the priest-savants, so proud of their perfect discernment, never grasped that the voices flooding their sanctuaries were not the heavens at all. By the time the great border-city burned and the murder of a Numinarian high oracle on Ar'an soil lit the war, the oracles of Numath-Vael were already speaking the Deceiver's words in the gods' own cadence, and calling it revelation.

## The War and the Silence

The war between the temple-hosts of Numinar and the Iron Banners of Ar ran for a decade across the borderlands, and every death fed the thing that had engineered it. The old gods, drawing their strength from the faithful now butchering one another in the mud, guttered and dimmed like lamps starved of oil.

For Numinar the breaking-point was not a battlefield but a revelation. Late in the war it was discovered, too late to matter, that the oracles had been compromised from the first, that the divine guidance which had steered the whole calamity had never come from the gods at all. The faith of a thousand years did not merely waver; it *collapsed*. A people who had built their entire civilization on the certainty that they heard the gods truly could not survive the discovery that they had heard a devil and knelt to it. The temple-hosts broke apart. Some priests recanted, some went mad, and some, most dangerous of all, kept preaching the poisoned revelations even after the poison was named, unwilling to believe that a lifetime of devotion had been an obscenity.

And then, in the war's final hour, the reckoning fell from the sky upon [[Ar]]. Numinar was spared the Sundering itself; the second sun struck the Ar'an heartland and not Numath-Vael. But Numinar was not spared its consequences. The monstrous waves came first, then the grey ash-fall that smothered the black fields for a season and killed the harvests that were Numinar's true heart. Worst of all was what could not be seen: with the old gods dead or dying and the well of worship poisoned at its source, the divine order that had held the eastern land safe simply *went out*, like a hearth-fire drowned. The bells of Numath-Vael, which had rung for a thousand years at the approach of the gods, fell silent all at once, and never rang again.

## The Blight

What came after has no proper name in the histories; the survivors called it only **the Blight**, and the maps that still trouble to mark Numinar mark it with that single word.

The poisoning of the well had done more than starve the gods. It had left a wound in the deep places of the world, and through that wound something *leaked*. The scholars who study the ley lines say of the [[Banner Sea]] that any spell cast above the drowned capital becomes twisted into some kind of abomination; in Numinar the same corruption seeped up not through water but through the black soil itself, into the ash-choked fields and the abandoned sanctuaries. The land did not merely die. It turned.

The first generation of horrors were the old temple-cities' own dead, and worse, their old *devotions*, gone rancid. Faith is a shape, and a shape does not vanish when its object dies; it festers. The prayer-carved stones of Numath-Vael, saturated with a millennium of worship and then with the Deceiver's poison, began to birth things: creatures of warped divine energy, malformed and hungry, that wore the half-remembered forms of the beings the oracles had once claimed to see. Where pilgrims had once flocked to the temple-roads, now things without proper names hunt them.

Into the emptied land came other monsters too, drawn as scavengers are drawn to a carcass. The great sanctuaries, built to be inviolable, made perfect lairs; the deep granary-vaults became warrens; and the highland rivers that had once made Numinar the bread basket of the world now run through a wasteland where nothing wholesome grows. Whole species found in no bestiary elsewhere in Æthernia are native only to the Blight, and the few surviving accounts agree that they grow stranger, and stronger, the closer one comes to the silent bells of Numath-Vael.

## The Scattering

Not all of Numinar died. In the war's last years, and in the ash-choked seasons after, those Numinarians who could flee did so, carrying what remained of their gold, their grain-seed, and their broken faith to any coast that would have them. Many carried nothing at all but the memory of a shame too large to speak of, a people who had knelt to a lie and, in kneeling, helped murder the heavens.

Some of these refugees found their way to the salt-bitten harbor of **Ar-Numath**, where the exiled and vindicated survivors of House Arn had built their remnant civilization. There, in time, the scattered faithful of dead Numinar and the outcast scholar-generals of dead Ar mingled, and it is from that grim union that the theologians of **Ar'num** descend, the same theologians who burned the Deceiver's true name from every record and who alone still keep the honest history of what was done to the Two Pillars. They remember Numinar not as it is, a wasteland crawling with the offspring of poisoned prayer, but as it was: the deepest well of worship in the world, and the most trusting.

## The Present

Numinar is now, by every honest account, uninhabitable. No nation claims it; no king levies it; the trade-roads that once carried its grain across the world end abruptly at the borders of the Blight and turn back. Adventurers and treasure-hunters are drawn to it despite everything, for a land of ten thousand temples is also a land of ten thousand unlooted altars, and the wealth of dead Numath-Vael is the stuff of legend. Few who go far into the Blight return, and fewer still return unchanged.

The eastern land keeps only one appointment now. On certain nights, travelers on the far borderlands swear they hear a single bell toll somewhere deep in the ruins, low and slow and wrong, as though the City of Ten Thousand Bells still tends its long-dead well, and something down there is still, patiently, answering the call to prayer.
